Atlas, for teams that work with AI agents

A plan your agents work from. Context they can ask for. Access they cannot exceed.

Atlas is where a team and its AI agents plan and track the work together: epics, stories and tasks, each tied to the part of your systems it changes. People see the plan on the board; agents read and write the same plan through Atlas's own MCP server, ask it for exactly the context a task needs, and reach only their person's access plus any grants of their own. It runs on your own server, starts with a map of itself, and you plan your work on top of that map.

What is MCP? The Model Context Protocol is an open standard that lets an AI assistant call tools on a server: "look this up", "file this task". Atlas ships an MCP server, so any agent that speaks MCP can read and write Atlas directly.

The problem

Working with several AI agents at once breaks the way most teams plan. Told plainly:

The plan lives in five chat windows.

You have three agent sessions open, each on its own piece. What each one is doing, and what is finished, exists only in its own conversation. Nobody, including you, can see the whole.

Agents stop on questions, and the questions get lost.

An agent reaches something only a person can decide and asks in its chat. If you are looking at another window, the question waits there, unseen, and the work stops with it.

The to-do list and the systems drift apart.

The description of your systems sits in a wiki, the work sits in a tracker, and within a week they disagree. Nothing tells you which open work touches which part of the system.

Every new session starts from zero.

Monday you explain how a service is put together and which rule nobody may break. Tuesday a new session knows none of it.

Nobody can say who may see what.

An agent given a shared key can read everything, and the log says "the bot did it", not whose agent, with which access.

Atlas makes each of these a property of the tool rather than a habit people are asked to keep.

One plan: epic, story, task

Work in Atlas has three levels:

  • an epic is a body of work with one outcome;
  • a story is a deliverable slice of it;
  • a task is one unit a person or an agent picks up.

A level may be skipped but never repeated or turned upside down, and Atlas refuses a move that would break that.

Each piece of work is tied to the one part of your systems it changes (Atlas calls this link works on). A task under a story that works on the search service works on it too, without saying so again. Select any part of the system and you see exactly the work that touches it.

What a piece of work carries:

  • acceptance criteria: the short list of statements that must be true for it to count as done;
  • a result: what was actually delivered, kept apart from the specification, so recording an outcome can never overwrite the plan;
  • a status, a priority, labels, and dependencies ("this waits on that");
  • an assignee, which is always a person.

Work rolls up. Finish a task and its story, its epic and the system above them all show the progress.

People and agents work the same plan

There is one plan, and people and agents both work in it.

  • An agent files work where it belongs. One call creates the item with its parent, its link to the system, its dependencies and its acceptance criteria, in one step that either happens completely or not at all. The server picks a task's number; an agent cannot invent one.
  • An agent works for a person. Work is assigned to people. An agent is its own account linked to one person and works on that person's behalf; its MCP tools cannot assign work.
  • Everything is signed. Every change records the account that made it, and the History tab on any item shows who changed what and on whose authority: a person, or a named agent acting for a named person.
  • Comments on work come from people and agents alike, each naming its author.
  • Sessions end with a sign-off. One call lists everything an agent wrote since a given time, with the steps for recording the session's work in the plan before the chat is closed. An operator can switch it off.

Decisions wait on a person, not in a chat

Some things are kept for people on purpose, and Atlas enforces it rather than asking nicely.

  • Acceptance criteria. An agent can propose new or changed acceptance criteria. The proposal appears on the item as a line-by-line comparison with the current list, and only a signed-in person can approve or reject it. An agent that tries to set the criteria directly is refused. Approving leaves two entries in the item's History: the proposal, by the agent on behalf of its person, and the approval, by the person.
  • Decision records. Larger decisions are kept as ADRs (architecture decision records: short documents that say what was decided, why, and what else was considered). They are searchable by people and agents, and listed on the project they belong to. A decision drafted by an agent, for example from the setup interview, stays Proposed until a person accepts it.
  • Where to look. What waits on a person today is a pending acceptance proposal, shown on the item it belongs to until someone approves or rejects it. Blocked work shows red, and in a chain of dependencies the work queued behind the red item shows orange. These colours are worked out from the plan, never painted by hand.

On the roadmap: a dedicated decision item, for a question an agent cannot answer alone. It hangs under the work it concerns, the work waiting on it points at it, it is always assigned to the person who must answer, and only that person (and their own agents) can see it. An administrator can open someone else's decision only through a separate, logged audit step that states its purpose. This design is agreed but not built yet.

The board

The board is where the plan is visible at a glance. It has three panes side by side, and a fourth you can open.

  • The graph: a radial picture of your systems, with each part's colour, progress ring and count of things that need a look.
  • The work board, showing the work for whatever you select in the graph, and everything inside it. It has three views: Epics (one card per epic with its progress), Board (columns by status, one lane per epic: Proposed, To do, In progress, Blocked, Done; drag a card to change its status) and Backlog (epic, story and task as a tree). Filter it to everyone's work, your own, or what nobody holds yet.
  • The node panel: one item's overview and acceptance criteria, its rules, comments, history, documents, links, and on a project its decision records.

Hover a card and the graph marks the system it works on; hover a system and the cards that touch it light up.

Attention, worked out for you. Each item's colour answers "do I need to look at this?", the ring answers "how done is it?", and a badge counts what below needs a look. In a chain of dependencies only one item is red: the one whose own dependencies are done, which is the one to do next; the items queued behind it are orange.

My dashboard: what is mine

The dashboard is a pane you open when you want it, made of boxes. Today it has one: My tasks, the open work assigned to you, in progress first, then by priority. Click a row and the board selects that item.

On the roadmap: boxes for the decisions waiting on you, and for the flow of work and what your agents are doing.

Atlas starts with a map of itself

A fresh install is not empty. It starts with a map of its own parts: the Atlas system and one node for each part (the API, the MCP server, the database, the gateway, search and retrieval), each with its documents. The handbook served at /docs is the shipped text of those same documents, so a new install's board, its agents and the handbook start out saying the same thing.

You plan your work on top of that map, and add the parts of your own systems beside it. The map is ordinary content: you, or your agents on your behalf, can change it as the system changes, and agents can keep it current with the same tools they use to file work. Your edits show on the board and through MCP; the handbook at /docs keeps showing the shipped text.

Opt-in: a richer starting map. Set ATLAS_SEED=self before the first start and the install begins with the world model Atlas keeps of itself: its parts, their documents, the links between them and a reviewed set of rules, with no work items.

What is a node? Atlas keeps everything in one graph: a set of nodes joined by labelled links (edges). Some nodes describe what exists (a system, a service, a database); others describe what is being done (epics, stories, tasks).

Going deeper: context your agents can ask for

The plan is half of it. The other half is that an agent never has to be told your systems again.

One call: "tell me about X"

An agent asks in its own words, and gets one bounded answer: the best-matching nodes, what sits around them, what would be affected by a change, the rules that apply, the decisions that bear on them, and the documents to read next. Each part is a short list of pointers with a cap on how many items it holds, and says when the cap cut it short; the answer also reports its own total size in bytes. The agent reads the pointers first and opens the full text only for what it needs, so a question costs little.

Search by words, and by meaning

  • Keyword search is built in, over names, descriptions, acceptance criteria, results, labels, decision records and documents. It answers with pointers, not pages.
  • Semantic search (search by meaning, not exact words) is an add-on you switch on and point at an embeddings service of your choice; no model ships with Atlas, and Atlas works fully without it.
  • Hybrid search combines the two: it takes the keyword ranking and the meaning ranking and merges them, so a result that does well on either rises. The "tell me about X" call uses hybrid by default, and falls back to keyword search with a note when the add-on is off.

What is an embedding? A list of numbers a language model computes for a piece of text, so that texts with similar meaning get similar numbers. It lets a search for "sign-in" find a document about "login".

Rules that every part inherits

A rule stated on a node is an intent: a constraint, a principle, a non-goal or an outcome. State it once on a system and every part inside it is bound by it. Rules roll down the way work rolls up. Agents are told to read the rules on what they change, inherited ones included, before they change it, and the shipped agent guide says to propose a change to a rule, not to make one.

Documents on nodes

How a part works, how to operate it, why it was built that way: documents live on the node they describe, with diagrams, and read the same on the board and through MCP. The handbook at /docs is the shipped text of Atlas's own documents.

Access they cannot exceed

Every agent has its own key, and it never exceeds its person

An agent is its own account, linked to a person. It signs in with its own token, never with the person's password, and what it writes is recorded against the agent, and so against that person. The token is limited by scope (read, write or admin), and the scope caps the role the agent can act with. The agent reaches its person's access, plus any grants of its own; it never takes on its person's role. Tokens expire, can be revoked at once, and are stored only as keyed fingerprints, so a copy of the database gives an attacker no working key.

What is a scope? A label on a token that says what kind of action it may take: read looks, write changes, admin deletes and manages access.

Who may see what: projects, roles and sign-in

In a team install, the project is the unit of access. Each person has a role on a project: viewer, editor, maintainer or owner, each allowed a little more than the one before. Roles can be given to groups, and those groups can come straight from your company sign-in. A project can be shared or kept private. If you may not see something, Atlas answers exactly as if it did not exist: no "access denied" that would confirm it is there, and nothing hidden is counted in a total or a search.

What are RBAC, SSO and OIDC? RBAC (role-based access control) means permissions come from the role you hold, not from a list kept per person. SSO (single sign-on) means people sign in with the account they already have at work. OIDC (OpenID Connect) is the standard that makes SSO work with providers such as Microsoft Entra ID or Keycloak.

Guardrails the agent cannot talk its way past

The API, not the agent, enforces them: a read token cannot write; no token can grant roles or mint another token; deleting through MCP is absent unless an operator turns it on, and then needs the admin scope; approving acceptance criteria and accepting a decision record need a person.

How it works

  • One graph in Postgres. Everything (the map, the work, rules, documents, comments, history) is rows in one database. No file feeds it.
  • One API decides. The board, the handbook, scripts and the MCP server all go through the same API, and it checks every request itself. The MCP server holds no rules of its own: it passes the agent's token on, and the API decides.
  • Behind any gateway. Atlas works with its own bundled TLS front, your reverse proxy, or an MCP gateway that gathers several tool servers behind one address. It trusts no identity a proxy claims, so a misconfigured edge can refuse requests but cannot widen anyone's access.
  • One machine, one port. One Linux virtual machine with Docker, one published https:// port, every hop that carries a token encrypted.

What is TLS? The encryption behind https://.

Who it is for

A person or a small team running several AI agents at once, who wants one plan those agents work from, with the questions only people can answer kept visible instead of lost in a chat.

Teams that self-host. One Linux virtual machine with Docker; your data stays in your Postgres database, on your server.

Teams at work, behind company sign-in. It signs people in through your identity provider, maps directory groups onto project roles, keeps a security log, and exports its audit trail for your security tooling.

It is probably not for you if you want a hosted service someone else runs, or a project tracker with no interest in AI agents.

Security and operations, in plain words

  • It is yours. Self-hosted: one virtual machine, your database, your sign-in. No hosted service behind it.
  • It does not phone home. The base install opens no connection out of its own networks once running, and the install test fails if anything tries. No analytics, no trackers. Outbound traffic happens only for what you switch on: semantic search, sign-in through your identity provider, and automatic public certificates if you choose that mode.
  • The gate fails closed. Every route is behind one check that denies by default, including routes added later. No switch turns it off and there is no default account.
  • Secrets are files, not settings. The install reads every secret from a file and refuses one given as an ordinary setting.
  • A supply chain you can check. Images pinned by digest; an SBOM for every image and lock; a scan fails the build on any fixable critical or high CVE; runtimes near end of life fail it too.
  • Honest limits. Atlas does not defend against someone with root on the host, and it does not judge an agent's intent: an agent can do what its access allows. The controls for that are narrow scopes, short expiry, instant revocation and the audit trail.

What are SBOM and CVE? An SBOM (software bill of materials) is a list of every component and version inside a piece of software. A CVE is a publicly catalogued security flaw. Together they let you answer "are we affected?" in minutes.

Getting started

What you need: a 64-bit Linux VM with Docker Engine and Docker Compose v2, 2 vCPU, 4 GiB of memory (2 GiB minimum) and 20 GiB of disk plus room for backups; a DNS name; port 443 free. Sized from a measured install run, not an estimate.

  1. Clone and configure. Copy deploy/oss/.env.example to .env and put your DNS name in it.
  2. Make the secrets and certificates with scripts/init-secrets.sh, which never prints a secret. Or bring your own certificates.
  3. Start it with docker compose up -d --build --wait.
  4. Create the first owner with atlas-admin user add <name> --role owner. It prints a temporary password, once.
  5. Sign in and set your password. Open your address, sign in with the temporary password and choose your own: Atlas sends you to the change form first. The board opens with Atlas's own map already there; file your first epic on top of it.
  6. Connect your agent. Mint a token into a file, point the Claude Code template at your install, and add it. Any MCP client that can send a bearer token works the same way.
  7. Add what you need, when you need it (optional): the MCP gateway, semantic search, single sign-on.

INSTALL.md has nine sections: the prerequisites, then the required steps (configure, secrets, start, first owner, connect Claude Code) and three optional ones (gateway, semantic retrieval, single sign-on). Scheduled backups are set up after install, in the operations guide.

FAQ

Does Atlas run my agents?

No. Your agents run where they run today, in Claude Code or any MCP client. Atlas is the plan they work from, the context they ask for and the gate on what they may touch.

How do my agents and I share the work?

Work is assigned to people. Your agent works on your behalf: it files and updates work, comments, and proposes acceptance criteria; you approve, assign and decide. Every change shows who made it.

Does any of our data leave our network?

Not with the base install, and the install test proves it. Semantic search and single sign-on call out only to the services you configure for them.

Which AI agents work with it?

Any agent that speaks MCP and can send a bearer token. A ready-made pack is included for Claude Code. Scripts can use the REST API directly.

Can an agent see more than I can?

Only what someone grants the agent itself. An agent reaches its person's access plus any grants of its own, never its person's role, and its token's scope caps what it may do. Remove the person's access and the agent loses it on its next call.

Can we use our company sign-in?

Yes, through OIDC (Microsoft Entra ID, Keycloak or any OpenID provider), with directory groups mapped onto project roles and one break-glass account.

What does it need to run?

One Linux VM with Docker: 2 vCPU and 4 GiB of memory recommended, 2 GiB as the floor.

How do we back it up and upgrade it?

A backup command takes a dump and proves it restores before it counts; it can run on a schedule. An upgrade is a new release, a rebuild and a schema step that never changes existing data.

Who owns it, and can we change it?

It is open source under Apache-2.0, so you may run, change and redistribute it under that licence.
